Practical garden record

Track plant growth stages without turning it into homework

Record a change when you can see it. A date, one photo and a short note are usually enough to explain a plant's season later.

Reviewed September 10, 2026

A useful growth tracker answers two questions: what phase is the plant in now, and what changed since the last entry? You do not need daily measurements or a perfect botanical model.

Start with this one-line record

Date + phase + visible change + care or conditions + photo.

Example: “June 18. First flower. Two buds opened after a hot week. Watered when the potting mix felt dry. Added one front-facing photo.”

Choose phases that match the plant

Plants do not all follow the same sequence. An annual tomato moves from seed to harvest in one season. A perennial may repeat vegetative growth, flowering and dormancy for years. Use only the labels that help you recognize a meaningful change.

  1. Planted or acquired: record the seed date, transplant date or the day you brought the plant home.
  2. Germination: use this when the seedling first emerges. Skip it for a purchased plant.
  3. Seedling or establishment: note early leaves, rooting or recovery after transplanting.
  4. Vegetative growth: record new stems, leaves, height or spread.
  5. Budding and flowering: separate these if the time from bud to open flower matters to you.
  6. Fruiting or harvest: record fruit set, ripening and what you picked. For non-fruiting plants, skip this phase.
  7. Dormancy or end of season: use this for plants that rest, die back or finish their life cycle.

What to record at each change

Write the date and phase first. Then add the observation that made you change the phase. Useful details include plant height, leaf count, the first bud, the first open flower, fruit set, pest damage, pruning, fertilizer, rainfall and harvest weight.

Keep care and weather next to the observation when they may help you interpret it. Avoid claiming that one event caused another unless you tested it. “New growth appeared four days after feeding” is a record. “The fertilizer caused the growth” is a conclusion the record alone cannot prove.

Take photos you can compare

Use roughly the same angle and distance each time. Include a familiar object or ruler when scale matters. A weekly photo is enough for steady growth; take an extra one when the plant enters a new phase, develops a problem or recovers.

Example: one patio tomato

  • April 12, planted: one seed in a labeled cell.
  • April 19, germination: shoot visible above the mix.
  • May 10, seedling: several true leaves; moved to a larger pot.
  • June 16, flowering: first cluster opened; photo from the same side as the May entry.
  • June 28, fruiting: first small green fruit visible.
  • August 3, harvest: first ripe tomato picked; weight recorded.

These dates are an example record, not a growing schedule. Variety, starting method, weather and location all change the timing.

Use a notebook or the free template

One page per plant works well on paper. In a spreadsheet, use one row per dated milestone. The free CSV opens in Excel, Numbers, Google Sheets and most spreadsheet apps.

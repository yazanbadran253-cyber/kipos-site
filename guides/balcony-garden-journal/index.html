Small-space gardening guide

A balcony garden journal you will keep using

Record the conditions that change quickly in containers: soil moisture, sun, wind, heat, care and the plant's response.

Reviewed September 10, 2026

A balcony compresses a garden into a small area, but the pots may behave very differently. A dark container against a hot wall can dry faster than a larger pot in afternoon shade. Your journal should help you notice those differences.

The smallest useful entry

Date + plant + soil check + action + visible change.

Example: “July 9. Basil in the blue 3-gallon pot. Top inch dry at 8 a.m.; watered until drainage. New leaves look firm. No pests seen.”

Set up one record per container

Start with the plant name and variety, container size, drainage, potting mix, balcony position and planting or purchase date. Give the pot a short name such as “rail box west” or “blue pot by door.” That name makes later entries much easier to match.

Oregon State University Extension recommends containers with drainage holes and notes that container plants lose water and nutrients faster than plants in the ground. Pot size, sun and wind all affect how quickly the mix dries.

Check moisture before you log watering

Do not turn a calendar entry into an automatic instruction to water. Check the potting mix first. Oregon State advises watering containers when the soil feels dry and continuing until water begins to leave the drainage holes. Penn State also recommends checking moisture with a finger before watering.

Your entry can be as short as “dry, watered” or “still moist, skipped.” Add time of day during hot weather. This separates a reminder from the decision the plant and soil conditions require.

Record changes, not routine

You do not need a paragraph every day. Make an entry when you plant, move a pot, prune, feed, spot pests, see flowers, harvest or notice stress. Add rain, unusually hot days and strong wind when those conditions help explain what happened.

A photo from the same position every week or two gives you a visual baseline. Add an extra photo when a leaf problem begins, after treatment, or when the plant changes phase.

A weekly five-minute check

  1. Look at each container's drainage and soil moisture.
  2. Note a new phase or visible change.
  3. Record feeding, pruning, pest treatment or harvest since the last check.
  4. Take one photo only when it adds useful evidence.
  5. Write one adjustment for the coming week, if the record supports one.

Example: three entries that explain a problem

  • July 2: pepper moved from the east wall to the exposed railing. Soil moist; no water.
  • July 4: hot and windy. Pot dry by late afternoon; watered until drainage.
  • July 6: leaf edges curled. Soil dry in the morning; moved pot back from the railing and photographed the leaves.

The notes do not diagnose the plant. They preserve the timing and conditions, which gives you better information for a trusted garden source or local extension service.
